How to Play American Mahjong: A Beginner's Guide

Four players pass and trade tiles, then draw and discard until someone completes one of the hands printed on that year's card. That's the whole game. Everything else is detail.

The 60-second version

  • Four players sit at a square table. Each has a rack to hold tiles.
  • The tiles are washed (shuffled face-down), built into walls and dealt. The dealer, called East, starts with 14 tiles. Everyone else starts with 13.
  • Everyone looks at the current year's card, published by the National Mah Jongg League (NMJL). It lists every hand that can win this year.
  • Before regular play, players swap unwanted tiles in a fixed sequence called the Charleston.
  • Then play moves around the table. On your turn you draw one tile and discard one tile.
  • When someone discards a tile you need to finish a group of three or more, you can call it, with some limits.
  • The first player whose 14 tiles exactly match a hand on the card declares Mahjong and wins the hand.

If you only remember one thing: in American Mahjong, you don't invent your own winning hand. You build one that's already printed on the card.

What you need

  • An American Mahjong tile set. It needs at least 152 tiles, including 8 Jokers. Most sets come with a few extras. A 144-tile Chinese set won't work because it has no Jokers.
  • Four racks, one per player, to hold your tiles upright and hidden.
  • Pushers, the arms used to slide your wall toward the middle of the table. Many modern racks have them built in.
  • Two dice for choosing the dealer and breaking the wall.
  • A current NMJL card for each player. The card changes every year, so last year's won't do. You can order it from the National Mah Jongg League.

Meet the tiles

An American set has seven kinds of tiles. You'll recognize them quickly after a game or two.

Two small things that trip up beginners:

  • Each Dragon is linked to a suit: Green goes with Bams, Red with Craks, and White (Soap) with Dots. The card uses this when a hand calls for "matching" Dragons.
  • The White Dragon also works as a zero in hands that spell out a year, like 2026.

Beginner tip: Numbers on Crak tiles can be hard to read if you don't know Chinese characters. Many sets print small Arabic numerals in the corner. If yours doesn't, keep a reference nearby until the characters feel familiar.

Set up the table

Every hand starts the same way. All the tiles go face-down in the middle and everyone mixes them around. This is called washing. Then each player builds a wall 19 tiles long and 2 tiles high in front of their rack. Four walls of 38 tiles use all 152 tiles.

Players roll dice to decide who starts as East, the dealer. East rolls again to decide where to break the wall, then everyone takes tiles in turn until East has 14 and the others have 13.

It sounds like a lot in writing. At the table it takes a couple of minutes. The step-by-step version is in our table setup guide.

Learn to read the card

The card is a list of every winning hand for the year, grouped into sections. Each line shows a pattern of numbers and letters. A few basics unlock most of it:

  • Colors show how many suits to use, not which suits. A line printed in one color uses one suit. Two colors means two different suits. You pick which ones.
  • Repeated characters show group size. "11" is a pair, "111" is three of a kind (a Pung), "1111" is four (a Kong).
  • Letters stand for honor tiles: N, E, W, S for Winds, D for Dragon, F for Flower.
  • X or C on the right side tells you whether you can call discards for that hand (X, exposed) or must build it from the wall (C, concealed).

Read our full guide to reading the NMJL card before your first game. It's the single biggest help.

The Charleston

Right after the deal and before anyone draws, players swap tiles in a fixed order. You pass three tiles you don't want and receive three from another player.

  1. First Charleston (required): pass right, then across, then left.
  2. Second Charleston (optional): pass left, then across, then right. Any one player can choose to stop before it starts.
  3. Courtesy pass (optional): trade up to three tiles with the player across from you.

You can never pass a Joker. The Charleston is where most beginners feel rushed, so we wrote a full guide with a diagram: How the Charleston works.

What happens on your turn

After the Charleston, East starts by discarding one tile. Play then moves to the right, counterclockwise around the table. On a normal turn you:

  1. Draw the next tile from the wall.
  2. Look at your hand and the card. Does this tile help?
  3. Exchange a Joker if you can. If someone has an exposed Joker standing in for a tile you're holding, you may swap your tile for it.
  4. Discard one tile face-up in the middle and say its name out loud, like "Four Bam" or "North."

You should end every turn with 13 tiles. Counting now and then is a good habit. Having the wrong number of tiles can make your hand dead.

Calling a tile

When another player discards a tile, you can claim it if it completes a group of three or more identical tiles that you need: a Pung, Kong, Quint and so on. Say "call" (or the tile's name) before the next player draws.

When you call a tile:

  • You place the completed group face-up on top of your rack. This is called exposing. Everyone can now see part of your hand.
  • You then discard, and play continues to your right. Turns in between are skipped.

Two limits matter most for beginners:

  • You can't call a discard to make a pair or a single tile, unless that tile completes your whole hand for Mahjong.
  • If the hand you're building is marked C (concealed) on the card, you can't call discards at all, except the final tile that wins the hand.

Rules note: What happens when two players call the same tile, and exactly when a call becomes final, can vary between home games and tournaments. Agree on it at your table, and check official NMJL materials for formal play.

Jokers

Jokers are wild, but only in groups of three or more identical tiles. They can never stand in for a single tile or a pair. That one rule causes more beginner mistakes than anything else.

A Joker exposed on someone's rack can be won by anyone who holds the real tile it's replacing. On your turn, swap your real tile in and take the Joker. All the details, including discarding Jokers, are in our Joker rules guide.

Winning

You win by completing exactly one hand on the current card. Every tile has to fit: the right numbers, the right number of suits, the right group sizes, and any conditions written in parentheses next to the hand.

You can complete your hand by drawing the final tile from the wall or by calling someone's discard. Say "Mahjong," lay your whole hand face-up and let the table check it against the card before anyone else throws in their tiles.

If nobody completes a hand before the wall runs out, the hand is a draw, often called a wall game. Everyone washes the tiles and starts again.

Many groups play for small amounts of money using the values on the card, and others simply play for fun. That's entirely up to your table. Our game night guide covers the common conventions.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Committing too early. Picking one exact hand the moment the tiles are dealt makes the Charleston harder. Aim for a section of the card first and narrow down as tiles arrive.
  • Misusing Jokers. Using a Joker in a pair, or calling a discard to make a pair, will kill your hand.
  • Losing count of tiles. Forgetting to discard after drawing, or knocking a tile off your rack, leaves you with the wrong number.
  • Ignoring X and C. Calling a discard for a concealed hand gives it away and can make your hand dead.
  • Panicking in the Charleston. It's fine to ask the table to slow down. Beginners are entitled to time.

Most of these end the same way: a dead hand. It happens to everyone, and it's one of the fastest ways to learn.
