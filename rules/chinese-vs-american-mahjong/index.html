What Is the Difference Between Chinese and American Mahjong?

They share tiles and a draw-and-discard rhythm, but American Mahjong adds Jokers, the Charleston and an annual card of winning hands. In practice they play like different games.

If you learned Mahjong from family or while traveling, you may sit down at an American table and feel lost despite knowing the tiles. That's normal. Below are the four differences that matter most. We use "Chinese Mahjong" here to mean traditional Chinese-style rules; there are many regional versions, and they differ from one another too.

1. The card vs. standard patterns

In traditional Chinese Mahjong, a common winning structure is four melds and a pair. The melds can be sequences (Chows), three of a kind (Pungs) or four of a kind (Kongs), in many combinations.

American Mahjong works differently. Players build one of the hands shown on the current annual card from the National Mah Jongg League. Your hand has to match a line on that card exactly. Because the card changes every year, the best strategy changes with it.

If you're coming from Chinese rules, the card is the biggest adjustment. Our guide to reading the NMJL card will help.

2. The Charleston

American Mahjong starts every hand with the Charleston: a series of passes where players trade three unwanted tiles at a time with their neighbors. It's borrowed from card games like Hearts.

Traditional Chinese Mahjong has no passing phase. Once the tiles are dealt, drawing and discarding begin, so the opening depends more on the luck of the deal. See how the Charleston works.

3. Jokers

If you open a Mahjong case and see Jokers, it's an American set. American sets add 8 Jokers, bringing the total to 152.

Jokers matter because the American card often asks for five or six identical tiles. There are only four of any real tile, so those hands are impossible without wild tiles. Jokers can only be used in groups of three or more, never in a single or a pair. The full rules are in our Joker guide.

4. Calling discards

In Chinese Mahjong you can usually claim the discard of the player to your left to complete a sequence of three consecutive numbers in one suit.

American Mahjong has no Chow. You can only call a discard to complete a group of identical tiles (three or more), or to complete your whole hand for Mahjong. Hands on the card that use consecutive numbers are built from groups and single tiles, not called sequences.

Can you use a Chinese set for American Mahjong?

Not without changes. A Chinese set has no Jokers, and American hands frequently can't be completed without them. Some players add aftermarket Joker stickers to blank tiles, but if you're buying new, get an American set. Our set buying guide explains what to check.

Which should you learn?

Learn whichever your friends or local group play. In the United States, most community center, library and club games play American Mahjong with the NMJL card. Elsewhere, and in many families, Chinese or other regional rules are the norm. Both are worth knowing, but try not to learn them at the same time.
