How to Read the NMJL Mahjong Card: A Beginner's Guide

The colors tell you how many suits to use, the repeated numbers tell you group sizes, the letters are honor tiles, and X or C tells you whether you can call discards.

Updated September 2026 / 4 min read / MahjongCenter Editorial Team

The first time someone hands you the card, it looks like a wall of colored numbers and letters. It's actually a map with a small legend. Once you know the legend, you can read any line in seconds.

About the card: The card is published by the National Mah Jongg League and changes every year. MahjongCenter doesn't reproduce it or list its hands. The examples below are invented to show the notation and aren't from any card. You'll need your own current card to play.

How the card is organized

The card groups hands into sections, each built around a theme, such as hands that use the year, even numbers, consecutive numbers, or Winds and Dragons. Each line in a section is one complete winning hand of 14 tiles.

On the right side of each line you'll find two things: a letter (X or C) and a value. The value is what the hand is worth if your group keeps score.

?

Beginner tip: Don't try to memorize lines. Learn the sections first. When you look at your tiles, ask "which section do these belong to?" before "which line?"

The golden rule of colors

Colors on the card don't stand for specific suits. Red doesn't mean Craks. Green doesn't mean Bams. This is the single most common beginner mix-up.

The colors tell you how many different suits the hand uses. You choose which suits.

If the numbers on a line useThen your hand usesExample choices
One colorOne suit throughoutAll Dots, or all Bams, or all Craks
Two colorsTwo different suitsOne color's groups in Craks, the other color's in Dots
Three colorsAll three suitsOne of each: Bams, Craks and Dots

Winds and Flowers don't belong to any suit, so the color they're printed in doesn't limit your choice.

Numbers and group sizes

Numbers written together form one group. The number of times a digit repeats is how many identical tiles you need:

5Single tile
55Pair (2)
555Pung (3)
5555Kong (4)
55555Quint (5)
555555Sextet (6)

Spaces separate one group from the next. Remember the Joker rule while you read: Jokers can fill in groups of three or more, never singles or pairs. So on any line, the singles and pairs have to be real tiles. That's why pairs matter so much when you choose a hand. More on Jokers.

Letters and symbols

  • N, E, W, S are the Winds: North, East, West, South.
  • D is a Dragon.
  • F is a Flower. In American Mahjong all Flowers are interchangeable, so any Flower counts.
  • 0 is the White Dragon (Soap) used as a zero, most often in hands that spell out the year.

Which Dragon goes with which suit?

When the card asks for a Dragon that "matches" a suit, use these pairings:

  • Bams match Green Dragons
  • Craks match Red Dragons
  • Dots match White Dragons (Soap)

A Dragon printed in the same color as a group of numbers is generally the Dragon that matches that group's suit. A Dragon in a different color is generally a Dragon from another suit. Check the notes on your card, since wording can vary from year to year.

X vs. C: exposed or concealed?

At the far right of each line is an X or a C. It decides how you're allowed to build that hand.

X (Exposed)C (Concealed)
Can you call discards for groups?Yes, for groups of three or moreNo
Can you call the tile that wins?YesYes
Where your groups sitCalled groups go face-up on top of your rackEverything stays hidden until you win

Calling a discard while the only hand you could be making is concealed is one way a hand goes dead.

Always read the parentheses

Next to many lines is a short note in parentheses. It often changes how flexible the hand is. Notes can say things like whether you may use any run of consecutive numbers, any matching numbers, or which Dragons to use.

Here's an invented example of why it matters. Imagine a line showing groups of 1s, 2s, 3s and 4s with a note allowing any consecutive run. That note means you aren't locked into 1-2-3-4; a run like 5-6-7-8 would count too. Without reading the note, you might throw away tiles you could have used.

When a note and your assumption disagree, the note wins.

A simple way to practice

  1. Pick one section of your card and read every line in it out loud, naming actual tiles ("pair of Norths, three 2 Bams...").
  2. Build one line with real tiles on the table. Then build it again in different suits.
  3. Deal yourself 13 tiles and find the two or three sections they fit best.

Ten minutes of this before your first game makes the Charleston far less stressful.

Where to get the current card

Order it directly from the National Mah Jongg League. New cards typically arrive in spring, and many groups switch to the new card as soon as everyone has theirs. Each player needs their own. Large-print versions are also offered.
