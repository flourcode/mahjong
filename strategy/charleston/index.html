How the Charleston Works in American Mahjong

Pass three tiles right, across and left. If everyone agrees, pass left, across and right. Then you may trade up to three tiles with the player across. Jokers never move.

Updated September 2026 / 5 min read / MahjongCenter Editorial Team

Quick answer: The first Charleston goes right, across, left, three tiles each time. An optional second Charleston goes left, across, right, and any player can stop it. An optional courtesy pass of up to three tiles across ends it. You can never pass a Joker.

The Charleston happens right after the deal and before anyone draws from the wall. It's borrowed from card games like Hearts, and it's unique to American Mahjong. Good passing can shape your whole hand, which is why many players think of it as the most important skill in the game. It's covered briefly in our beginner's guide; this is the full version.

The Charleston at a glance

Every pass is exactly three tiles, except blind passes (where some of your three may come from the incoming pass) and the courtesy pass (zero to three).

Stage 1: The first Charleston (required)

Everyone takes part in the first Charleston.

  1. First right. Pass three tiles you don't want to the player on your right. You'll receive three from the player on your left.
  2. First across. Pass three tiles to the player opposite you, and receive three from them.
  3. First left. Pass three tiles to the player on your left, and receive three from your right. This is the last pass of the round, so a blind pass is allowed.

Place your outgoing tiles face-down in front of the player you're passing to, and wait until they've passed before picking up yours. It keeps things tidy and fair.

Stage 2: The second Charleston (optional)

After the first Charleston, the table decides whether to do a second. If any one player wants to stop, there's no second Charleston. You don't have to give a reason, and nobody should ask.

If everyone continues, the direction reverses:

  1. Second left. Three tiles to your left.
  2. Second across. Three tiles across.
  3. Second right. Three tiles to your right. This is the last pass, so a blind pass is allowed again.

Beginner tip: Players often stop the second Charleston because their hand is already coming together and they don't want to give anything else away. If someone says stop, that's a small clue about their hand.

Stage 3: The courtesy pass (optional)

Once the Charlestons are over, you and the player across from you may agree to trade zero, one, two or three tiles. If you want to trade two and they want to trade one, you both trade one. There's no blind passing here, and Jokers still can't be passed.

After the courtesy pass, East discards to begin regular play.

The blind pass

Sometimes you reach the last pass of a round and don't have three tiles you're willing to give up. The blind pass solves that.

On the first left and second right, you may take one, two or all three of the tiles being passed to you in that same round and pass them straight on without looking at them, adding enough of your own tiles to make three. Players also call this "stealing."

When it helps: your hand is already well formed and giving up a third tile would break a promising group or pair. Passing unseen tiles protects your hand while still following the rules.

House rules vary: Tables sometimes differ on the exact mechanics of the blind pass, including which incoming tiles you can pass on. Ask before the game if you're new to a group.

Charleston strategy: choosing your hand

Aim for a section, not a line

Beginners often pick one exact line on the card as soon as they see their tiles. Better players aim for a section of the card, like even numbers or consecutive runs, and let the tiles that arrive decide the exact line. You stay flexible, and you're less likely to pass away something you'll need.

Let your pairs lead

Jokers can fill in groups of three or more, but never pairs. So the real pairs you hold are often what decides which hands are actually possible. When you're unsure between two sections, favor the one your pairs support. Why Jokers can't make pairs.

Spot your passers early

Before the first pass, find the tiles that don't connect to anything: lone Winds, single numbers far from your others, a suit you're not using. Those are your first passes.

Count your Jokers

If you're dealt several Jokers, hands with large groups become much more realistic. If you have none, lean toward hands built on real tiles you already hold.

Defensive passing: what not to give away

The Charleston isn't only about your hand. Every tile you pass might help someone else.

  • Don't pass a pair to the same player. Two identical tiles hand them a pair, and possibly the start of a Pung or Kong. Split them up across different passes if you must pass them.
  • Avoid passing tiles that work together. A 1, 2 and 3 of the same suit, or three different Winds, are easy for someone to use.
  • Mix it up. An ideal pass is three unrelated tiles, such as a Wind, a low number in one suit and a high number in another.

Charleston questions

Can you pass a Joker during the Charleston?

No. Jokers can't be passed in any part of the Charleston, including the courtesy pass.

Can the second Charleston be stopped?

Yes. It only happens if all four players agree. Any one player can stop it.

What is a blind pass?

On the last pass of each Charleston, passing along some or all of the tiles coming to you in that pass without looking at them, so you don't have to break up your own hand.

Do you have to pass exactly three tiles?

Yes, in both Charlestons, though some of the three may be blind-passed on the last pass of a round. The courtesy pass is the only one where you can pass fewer than three.

What if I pass the wrong number of tiles?

Fix it before anyone looks at their new tiles if you can. Most friendly tables simply correct the mistake. Ending up with the wrong number of tiles once play starts is a bigger problem, since it can make your hand dead.

How long should the Charleston take?

Experienced tables move quickly, but beginners should be given time. If you're teaching, our guide to teaching a beginner suggests never rushing a new player here.
